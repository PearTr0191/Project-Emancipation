The best answer I've seen was a boy carrying a chair.

Last August, twenty-three freshmen were sitting beneath ceiling fans pushing warm air. I ran it the way I run everything: a laminated run sheet, games ranked by noise level, a backup for rain that never got used. It worked for the first hour. Where do I print my schedule? Which gate for the assembly? The answers took milliseconds. I was proud of that.

A girl in the third row leaned over her desk. "I don't know anyone here," she said. "Was it like that for you?" The room went quiet except for the fans, and twenty-three faces came to me. The run sheet's corner bit my palm. I had a backup for rainy days and not one for this. I gave her the study-group list and the buddy board, everything true and none of it an answer.

A boy in the back row picked up his plastic chair, carried it to her table, and sat down. "Save me a seat tomorrow." She laughed. The room exhaled and moved on without me.

My own answer to her took until Thursday at one in the morning. The entry starts with I should have carried the chair. It solves nothing. Four days after she asked, it went into the journal with the others.

Two weeks into senior year, our class monitor tagged everyone in the chat, saying she had raised the scores of anyone under 9. Then came "đừng ý kiến nhé...!", Vietnamese for don't comment. I read it at 15:21 and left a heart. She asked for none, and I have never been better at that. My friends have a phrase for it: seen, no rep.

The count says the rest: 214 messages, all read and four answered, all four logistics. In the software I build, a request comes in and, gets logged "200 OK" and the silence that follows is not a wound. Between people, delivered only means it landed, and hers is one of the replies I still owe.

The delay goes back further than the chat, to a CircleK on a cold evening in eleventh grade, when I was late for extra class and my score report was an hour old, glowing in my pocket. In line for an ice shake I understood I had no one to send it to. The score was real. The room stayed the same. I went home and wrote it down.

For two weeks an English test score put my face in the newspapers and on national television. Congratulations came in as "ghê," Vietnamese for impressive. Kind, and quick, and nowhere near. The gap didn't move an inch. I wrote that down too.

That entry ends with a line I have not managed to improve: "how far I am from everyone, no matter direction."

This summer a stranger messaged my app ViDrive and asked whether he could afford a Honda CR-V. The free calculators were tied to individual brands, so I built my own. He doubted the savings of an electric car; the numbers gave him something firmer than instinct. He wrote back that the calculation showed 330 million dong in savings, over $13,200, and changed his decision.

Four minutes from his question to my answer, while my friends waited on last week's messages. For once someone answered me. I opened the now 450-unread class chat and replied to the oldest message in it. It took eleven minutes.

ViDrive holds itself to a five-percent mean absolute percentage error. When the model misses, the app flags it and shows its work. When I miss, I go quiet. For years I've run the test on the distance between me and the people I love, and it hasn't landed within tolerance.

So I'm learning to flag mine: I still read every message, and these days I answer some.
