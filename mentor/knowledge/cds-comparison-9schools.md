## What each grid means for your profile

Your Tier 1 asset is **ViDrive** — a shipped, quantified product (build/do, not a competition award or art/athletic talent). Your score is **1540 with 800 Math**. You are a **Vietnamese international** needing large scholarships on a **–20k budget**. Read each grid through those three lenses.

### Where ViDrive carries the most weight

Only **Stanford** and **Dartmouth** rate extracurricular activities as Very Important. At every other school on this list, your activities are Important or Considered — supporting, not central. That is not a reason to downgrade ViDrive; it means the *role* it plays changes. At Stanford/Dartmouth it is a primary differentiator. At the targets/safeties, your transcript and score do more of the work — and your score is genuinely strong relative to them.

**Stanford** is the clearest fit on paper: extracurriculars, talent, and character all Very Important, and your 800 Math sits at its 75th percentile. The mismatch is your composite sits below its median — which is true of every applicant at a 4% school, and the CDS does not tell you whether that specific gap is disqualifying.

**Dartmouth** is the second fit: nearly identical grid, but it demotes raw talent/ability to Important, and its admitted composite range sits ~30 points lower — meaning your score is an asset there, not a liability.

### Where your transcript does the heavy lifting

**Michigan** has the thinnest Very Important tier of any school here: only rigor of secondary school record and GPA. Everything else — essay, recommendations, test scores, extracurriculars, character — is Important or Considered. For an applicant with a strong upward GPA trend (G10 9.5 to G11 ~9.6–9.7) and a rigorous schedule, that grid is unusually readable. Your ViDrive is not wasted there, but it is not what the decision turns on.

**Georgia Tech** rates character/personal qualities Very Important alongside rigor and GPA — and it is one of the few schools here that explicitly elevates non-academic factors to the top tier. Its grid also rates state residency Very Important, which is a direct signal that it is built around Georgia residents: the headline admit rate overstates the odds for non-residents and internationals substantially. Test scores are only Considered at Georgia Tech despite being required — a strong score helps but will not rescue a thin transcript.

**Purdue** has the most builder-friendly target grid: essay Very Important (unusual for a large public — the essay is not a formality here), test scores Important, extracurriculars Important. Talent/ability is Not Considered, and there is no admissions interview. Its admitted SAT range (1220 to 1470) is the widest on this list and your 1540 is far above its 75th percentile — at Purdue your score is overwhelming evidence, not a marginal asset.

### The outlier worth special attention

**NYU** is the only school here that has actively reshuffled its C7 in a coherent direction: it demoted class rank, standardized test scores, and the interview (all things the applicant cannot fully control), and elevated the application essay, recommendations, and character/personal qualities (all things the file the applicant builds). That grid rewards what you can write and what teachers say about you — which, given your quantified shipped product and your upward trend, is a reasonably good match.

It is also the only school on this list confirmed to **meet full demonstrated financial need for first-year students**. On a –20k budget that is the single most financially relevant fact in this table — far more decision-relevant than the 9% admit rate. Verify the exact international-student packaging before treating it as decisive, but no other school here has a published need-meeting policy.

### The public/private and aid split

This table is not a financial aid table, and the knowledge base does not contain per-school aid generosity figures — that is a separate research task and should not be inferred from CDS data. What the CDS does tell you is that the publics (Michigan, Georgia Tech, Maryland, Purdue) weight **state residency**, which is irrelevant to you as an international applicant and is a signal that their grids are calibrated for domestic admits. It does not mean you are unwelcome there; it means the C7 is describing a process optimized for another population, and you should read the numbers accordingly.

### Honest limits of this exercise

- **CDS is self-reported and lagged.** These are the prior cycle snapshots.
- **C7 tells you what the school says it weighs, not your odds.** Two applicants at the same score get different decisions.
- **None of this captures fit.** Whether you would thrive at a given school is not in any section, and the supplementals are where that gets tested.
- **USC C7 is missing** from this table. Treat USC as a private with a ~33% SAT submit rate and a 1510 median — position it by reputation and supplementals, not by this grid.
- **Georgia Tech volunteer-work and work-experience rows** are reported inconsistently across sources (one reading puts them in the Very Important tier, the table above does not). Flagged rather than resolved.

## NYU financial aid — the one school where the budget math changes

Sourced from NYU's own financial-aid pages (nyu.edu/admissions/financial-aid-and-scholarships), not inferred from CDS data. The CDS contains no aid-generosity figures; this section is separate research.

**The NYU Promise (confirmed on NYU's site):**
- NYU meets **100% of demonstrated financial need** for every first-year undergraduate admitted to the New York campus, **regardless of citizenship status** — explicitly including international applicants and undocumented students.
- Families with income under $100,000 and typical assets **do not pay tuition**.
- Need is met through scholarships, grants, and expected student work — **loans are not included in the need-meeting package** (federal loans are offered separately on FAFSA; private loans are an option).

**Cost of attendance (2026-2027, NYU NYC campus, not yet officially approved as of the search date):**
- Tuition ~$65,622
- Housing, food, transportation ~$27,882
- Books and supplies ~$1,470
- **Total ~$96,988** (varies by school; Stern runs higher, Tisch runs higher due to equipment fees)
- NYU charges the **same tuition for international and domestic students** — no separate international rate.

**What an international student actually files:**
- **CSS Profile only** (College Board, $25 initial / $16 per additional school; fee-waiver codes available for international families through the NYU applicant portal). NYU school code: 2785.
- **No FAFSA** — federal aid (Pell Grants, subsidized loans) is reserved for U.S. citizens and eligible non-citizens, so an international student gets no federal dollars. The NYU Promise is funded entirely from NYU's own institutional budget.
- Financial aid deadlines: ED I = Nov 10, ED II = Feb 1, RD = Feb 1 (CSS Profile).

**Bottom line for a $15-20k budget:**
- NYU is the **only school on this list confirmed to meet full demonstrated need for first-years**, and that promise explicitly covers internationals. No other school here has a published equivalent.
- Whether it makes NYU affordable depends entirely on the CSS Profile's estimated family contribution. A Vietnamese family with modest assets and income will almost certainly get a large institutional grant — but the exact number cannot be known without filing. The CSS Profile fee-waiver path exists if cost is itself a barrier.
- **This is the single most budget-relevant fact in the entire comparison**, and it outweighs the 9% admit rate for decision-making purposes. It does not make NYU a safety — it makes it the only school on the list where the cost question is answerable in advance rather than discovered after an admit.
- **Verify before treating as decisive:** the 2026-2027 cost figures were not yet officially approved at the time of the search (NYU states they are finalized in July 2026), and the international packaging details should be confirmed with NYU's Office of Financial Aid directly (financial.aid@nyu.edu, 212-998-4444).
